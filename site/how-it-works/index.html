How it works

What happens to one satellite pass.

A pass lasts a few minutes and never comes back. Here is everything Meridian does with one, from deciding it is worth catching to telling the station's owner what happened.

Practice fleet Today these steps run on 50 practice stations Simulated. The first real station is being built.

  1. 1 · Predict

    Pick the passes worth catching.

    A satellite clears a station's horizon several times a day, and not every pass gives usable data. Meridian scores each one by how likely it is to work, its predicted yield, from how high it climbs, how fresh the orbit numbers are and what this station has managed before.

    What you seeA short list of passes, each with its chance of working and the reasons behind it.

  2. 2 · Choose

    Plan every station at once.

    With several stations and more passes than time, something has to decide who listens to what. Meridian makes one schedule for the whole network: the most promising passes are covered, no station is booked twice, and two stations do not spend a pass on the same satellite unless that helps.

    It is measured against simpler plans, such as always taking the highest pass, so the gain is a number rather than a claim.

  3. 3 · Receive

    Listen, and prove it.

    At the right moment the station tunes to the satellite and records. All the while it sends a heartbeat saying what it is doing. That is what tells "the station heard nothing" apart from "the station was switched off", two very different problems that would otherwise look the same.

    Why it mattersA station that was off has not missed a pass, and is not counted as having missed one.

  4. 4 · Check

    Say how sure we are the data is usable.

    The recording is decoded into the satellite's data, which for the Meteor-M weather satellites' LRPT signal is an image. Then Meridian says how likely the result is to be usable, its reception confidence.

    That percentage is tested against what really happened, so "80%" means about eight times in ten, not a feeling.

  5. 5 · Explain

    When a pass fails, say why.

    A lost pass gets a cause, worked out from the evidence: the patch of sky the station can see, its recent passes, its heartbeats and its clock. When the evidence does not support a cause, the answer is "undetermined", not a guess.

    What you see"Lost: the satellite set behind something to the north-east. Four of the last five passes there were lost too."

  6. 6 · Warn

    Catch a station getting worse.

    Antennas loosen, cables corrode and trees grow into the view. Meridian learns what normal looks like for each station and sends a health warning when it starts to slip, before the station fails outright.

  7. 7 · Tell and share

    Tell the owner, and publish the evidence.

    The station's owner gets a plain report: what was planned, what worked, what was lost and why. Passes the station never listened to are reported as that, and are not counted as misses.

    Everything behind the figures goes into an evidence dataset that anyone can download, rerun and check.

Pass

The few minutes a satellite spends above a station's horizon. Each pass happens once. If it is missed, its data is gone.

Every term

Predicted yield

Meridian's estimate of how likely a pass is to return usable data. It answers 'will this work?', not just 'will the satellite be visible?'.

Every term

Elevation

How high a satellite climbs above the horizon during a pass, in degrees, from 0 at the horizon to 90 straight overhead. Higher usually means a cleaner signal, but it is one clue among several.

Every term

Element set

The published numbers that describe a satellite's orbit, used to predict where it will be. They age: a week-old set predicts less accurately than yesterday's.

Every term

Schedule

Which station listens to which pass, decided for all stations at once, so the most promising passes are covered and no station is asked to be in two places.

Every term

Heartbeat

A short message a station sends regularly to say what it is doing. It is how Meridian tells a station that was listening apart from one that was switched off.

Every term

Decode

Turning a raw radio recording into the satellite's actual data. For a weather satellite, that means an image.

Every term

LRPT

The digital picture signal sent by the Meteor-M weather satellites on 137 MHz. It is what Meridian's first station is built to receive.

Every term

Reception confidence

How sure Meridian is that a reception's data is usable, as a percentage. It is checked against what really happened: of the passes marked 80%, about 80 in 100 should turn out good.

Every term

Loss cause

When a pass returns nothing useful, Meridian's best explanation: view blocked, radio interference, clock wrong, receiver off or satellite silent. When the evidence does not point anywhere, it says 'undetermined' instead of guessing.

Every term

Health warning

An early notice that a station is slowly getting worse, sent to its owner before it fails outright.

Every term

Evidence dataset

A sealed, downloadable copy of every pass and its result, with the settings used, so anyone can check Meridian's figures for themselves.

Every term

Meridian

Open software that decides which satellite passes are worth catching, catches them, checks the result and says what happened.
